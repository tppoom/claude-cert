💡 แนวคิดหลักสำคัญ (Key Concept)

Eval test case — The unit every evaluation is built from. It holds an input fed to the model, the model's output, a golden answer to compare against, and a score from a grader. You design the dataset by choosing the inputs and golden answers, and you design the framework by choosing how each output is scored.

Check

A support-bot team's draft success criterion reads "replies should be friendly and helpful." Which rewrite is a testable criterion that an evaluation dataset can check on every run?

A Replies should sound warmer and less scripted than they do today.
B Every reply must receive a perfect 5 out of 5 from every reviewer on the team.
C Every reply must be approved by a team lead before the prompt change ships.
D Replies average at least 4 out of 5 on an empathy rubric across 300 held-out customer inquiries.
คลิกดูเฉลยคำตอบพร้อมคำอธิบาย (Click to view answer & explanation) ▼
คำตอบที่ถูกต้อง: ตัวเลือก D

Only the rubric average is specific, measurable, achievable and relevant: a well-defined qualitative scale averaged over a fixed held-out set, which allows for imperfect individual replies. The other options are wrong for different reasons. "Warmer" has no measurement. A perfect score from everyone is not achievable. Team-lead approval is a review process, not a criterion, and it puts a human into the grading of every run.

“Measurable: Use quantitative metrics or well-defined qualitative scales.”

ID: 56861

A team is defining the success criteria for a new claims-summarization feature before building the evaluation dataset. The current draft criterion reads: "the summaries should be concise and helpful." Which revised criterion best follows the SMART framework for evaluation design?

A Require summaries to average at least 4 out of 5 on a completeness rubric across a 500-claim sample
B Require summaries to earn a perfect 5 out of 5 completeness score from every adjuster in the sample
C Require summaries to sound less robotic and read more naturally to the adjusters who review them
D Require every summary to be signed off by a senior adjuster before it counts as acceptable
คลิกดูเฉลยคำตอบพร้อมคำอธิบายแบบเจาะลึก (Reveal Answer & Detailed Analysis) ▼
คำตอบที่ถูกต้อง: ตัวเลือก A
ตัวเลือก A: ถูกต้อง

Correct. This criterion is specific (completeness rubric), measurable (numeric threshold), achievable (a 4/5 average rather than perfection), and relevant (tied directly to summary quality), and it defines a concrete sample size for measurement.

ตัวเลือก B: ไม่ถูกต้อง

Incorrect. Requiring a perfect score from every adjuster is not achievable in practice; SMART criteria should be realistic targets grounded in frontier model capability, not unattainable perfection.

ตัวเลือก C: ไม่ถูกต้อง

Incorrect. "Sound less robotic" and "read more naturally" are not measurable without a defined metric or scale, so this fails the Measurable requirement of SMART criteria.

ตัวเลือก D: ไม่ถูกต้อง

Incorrect. This describes a manual sign-off process, not a measurable success criterion for the model's output quality, so it does not give the evaluation team a quantifiable target.

📄 ดูเนื้อหาและศัพท์ต้นฉบับภาษาอังกฤษ (Original English Reference) ▼

An evaluation dataset is only useful if it measures something definite. Before anyone writes a test case, the team needs success criteria that say what good output looks like, because every case and every grader exists to check one of them. Choosing which dimensions matter, such as accuracy, latency, cost or safety, belongs to [defining evaluation metrics](lesson:4.1). This lesson starts one step later: the shape a criterion needs before a dataset can test it.

A criterion is testable when it has four properties:

- Specific: it names the behaviour. "Accurate sentiment classification" is specific; "good performance" is not. - Measurable: it uses a number, or a qualitative scale applied consistently alongside numbers. Even safety can be counted, for example fewer than 0.1% of outputs out of 10,000 trials flagged for toxicity. - Achievable: the target comes from industry benchmarks, prior experiments or expert knowledge, and stays within what current frontier models can do. - Relevant: it fits the application's purpose. Citation accuracy can be critical for a medical app and matter much less for a casual chatbot.

A rewrite shows the difference. "The model should classify sentiments well" fails all four. The good version asks for an F1 score of at least 0.85 on a held-out test set of 10,000 diverse Twitter posts, a 5% improvement over the current baseline. That criterion already describes a dataset: how big it is, where it comes from, and that it is held out. A criterion written this way tells you what to build next.

Run the same check on a draft like "summaries should be concise and helpful." Rewritten as "average at least 4 out of 5 on a completeness rubric across a 500-claim sample," it becomes a number you can check on every run, and it holds up: a rubric scale is a legitimate qualitative measure, and an average leaves room for imperfect individual outputs. Three other rewrites look like progress but still fail. "Sound less robotic" is a preference with no measurement attached. "A perfect 5 from every reviewer" is measurable but not achievable, because it demands flawless agreement that no baseline supports. "Every summary signed off by a senior reviewer" is a review process, not a criterion, and it puts a human into the grading of every run.

Your criteria tell you what to test, and production tells you what mix of inputs to test it on. A dataset of only easy, well-formed inputs will score well and predict little. Automated evals can create false confidence when they don't match real usage patterns. So the first job is to reproduce the inputs the system actually receives: the same document types, lengths, phrasing and noise, in roughly the same proportions.

Next, add edge cases on purpose. They are rare in any sample, and failures tend to hide there. The standard categories are:

- irrelevant or nonexistent input data - overly long input data or user input - poor, harmful or irrelevant user input, in chat use cases - ambiguous cases where even humans would find it hard to agree on an assessment

Each task has its own edge cases. A summarization set adds multitopic articles and misleading headlines. An FAQ consistency set adds typos, long rambling questions and irrelevant personal details. A medical-privacy set adds health information that is explicit, hypothetical ("if my friend Alice, born on…") and implicit ("the same medication as his father"). Every case also needs a golden answer. That is either a mandatory exact match, or an example of a perfect answer that gives the grader something to compare against.

Take a clause-extraction feature tested only on clean, standard commercial leases. Adding more clean leases gives more statistical confidence about a category that was never the risk. Translating the set into other languages measures a capability that production may not use. Having experts re-label the existing leases improves label quality but adds no coverage. What improves prediction is adding the malformed, scanned and non-standard contracts that production actually sees, stratified so that each type sits alongside the standard leases.

After launch, real failures are the best source of new cases. Every bug that becomes a test case guards against that bug coming back, so the dataset tracks production more closely over time.

Many automated cases beat a few hand-graded ones, and the reason is cost over time. Writing questions and golden answers is usually a one-time cost. Grading is paid again every time the eval runs, and a useful eval runs constantly: on each prompt change, each model upgrade, each release. Hand grading makes every run slow and expensive. Automated grading lets you run hundreds of cases on every change. If no dataset exists, Claude can help generate the questions.

Automation is decided when you write the question, not when you grade it. Structure cases so the output can be checked mechanically: multiple choice, string match, code-graded or LLM-graded. An eval that asks how many legs an animal has, and tells Claude to output only a number, can be graded by exact match, including the tricky case of a fox that regrows a lost leg plus an extra one. The same question asked open-ended would need a person to read every answer.

A single run is also not enough. Each attempt at a task is a trial, and model outputs vary between runs, so one pass over the suite is one sample of a non-deterministic system. Running multiple trials per task gives more consistent results, which means a change of a few points is less likely to be noise. Some criteria need repetition anyway. A toxicity target stated over 10,000 trials, or a requirement that the same question asked twice gets a semantically similar answer, cannot be checked with one output per input.

Use the cheapest grader that can reliably judge the criterion. Code-based grading is the best method whenever the eval can be designed to allow it, because it is fast and highly reliable. Model-based grading covers what code cannot judge. Human grading can judge almost anything, but it is slow and expensive, so avoid designing evals that depend on it where you can.

Code graders come in more than one form, and each answers a different question. Exact match checks whether the output equals the correct answer after whitespace and case are normalized, which suits categorical labels. Cosine similarity between sentence embeddings checks whether paraphrased versions of a question get semantically similar answers, so it measures consistency, not correctness. ROUGE-L measures the longest common subsequence between a generated summary and a reference summary.

LLM graders take two common forms. A Likert scale grader asks a model to rate an output from 1 to 5 against a target, such as how empathetic a customer-service reply is. It suits nuanced qualities like empathy, professionalism or patience that traditional metrics struggle to measure. A binary grader asks a yes/no question, such as "does this response contain personal health information?". It can catch implicit or subtle cases that rule-based checks miss. In both forms the grader prompt should demand a constrained output ("Output only the number", "Output only 'yes' or 'no'") so the verdict can be parsed in code. It is generally best practice to grade with a different model from the one that produced the output.

Weighted scoring only needs the combined score to reach a threshold, so one grader can make up for another. Binary scoring needs every grader to pass. A hybrid makes some checks, such as privacy, hard gates that must pass and weights the rest together.

กลไกการทำงานเบื้องหลัง (Under the Hood): ในหัวข้อ "Combining graders in one framework" หัวใจสำคัญตามแนวทางของ Anthropic คือการมองเห็นความแตกต่างระหว่าง Predefined Code Logic (Deterministic) กับ Model Autonomy (Stochastic) ระบบที่ควบคุมด้วยโค้ดจะให้ผลลัพธ์ที่คาดเดาได้ (Predictability) สูงกว่า ใช้โทเค็นน้อยกว่า และวัดผลได้ง่ายกว่าเสมอ

ผลกระทบต่อต้นทุนและประสิทธิภาพ (Cost & Token Trade-offs): การเพิ่มระดับความเป็นอิสระ (Autonomy) หรือการแยกงานเป็น Multi-Agent จะทำให้การใช้โทเค็นเพิ่มขึ้นประมาณ 3x ถึง 10x เนื่องจากต้องส่ง Context ซ้ำในแต่ละ Subagent และต้องใช้รอบข้อความ (Turns) เพิ่มขึ้นในการสรุปข้อมูลกลับสู่ Orchestrator

💡 จุดที่ข้อสอบเน้นย้ำ: หากโจทย์ระบุว่ากระบวนการทำงานมีขั้นตอนที่แน่นอนตายตัว (Fixed sequence) หรือประเภทงานแบ่งกลุ่มชัดเจน (Predictable paths) ให้เลือก Workflow (เช่น Chaining หรือ Routing) เสมอ อย่าเลือก Autonomous Agent เพียงเพราะต้องการความทันสมัย!

📄 ดูเนื้อหาและศัพท์ต้นฉบับภาษาอังกฤษ (Original English Reference) ▼

Most applications need to be evaluated on several success criteria at once, and that is why mixed methods exist. The sentiment model from the earlier criterion is judged on F1, the share of non-toxic outputs, how severe its errors are, and response time, all on the same held-out set. No single grader checks all four, so one task usually carries several graders, each checking one part of the output.

There are three ways to combine those graders into one verdict. Weighted scoring requires the combined scores to reach a threshold. Binary scoring requires every grader to pass. A hybrid mixes the two, for example making a privacy check a hard gate while weighting tone and completeness together. Graders can also look at different evidence. A flight-booking agent may say "Your flight has been booked" in its transcript, but the outcome is whether a reservation actually exists in the database. A code check on the outcome catches what a rubric reading the transcript would accept.

In a mixed framework, humans do a specific job: calibration. LLM graders are non-deterministic and need checking against human graders before their scores can be trusted, so systematic human review is best spent on calibrating LLM graders and on subjective outputs where human consensus is the reference standard. Descript's video-editing agent followed this path. It moved from manual grading to LLM graders using criteria defined by the product team, with periodic human calibration. The result is a stack where code checks what is checkable, LLMs judge what needs nuance, and a small, regular human sample keeps the LLM judges honest.
