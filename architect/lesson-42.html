Testable requirement — A requirement worded precisely enough that someone outside the project could look at the finished system and say whether it passes or fails. Each stage of discovery aims to turn a vague request into requirements of this kind.

1. The discovery brief: users, needs, experience, constraints

Structured discovery is the work you do before designing anything

You collect a fixed set of facts about who the system serves and what limits it, so every later decision can be checked against them

A request such as "patients need to check wait times before coming in" is where the conversation starts, not a requirement

To turn it into a brief, answer four questions in plain language:

  • ▸
    Users. Be specific. Which patients? What about the staff? Who else touches the system?
  • ▸
    Needs. What outcome are they hoping for? This is not the same as the feature they asked for.
  • ▸
    Experience. What does a great solution feel like to use? Describe the experience, not the features.
  • ▸
    Constraints. Budget, the technical skill of the people who will run it, users' access to devices, and privacy requirements.

A process map is the discovery technique that keeps the brief honest

The idea that [you start from the business process, not the model](lesson:1.1) becomes a concrete step in discovery

Break the user's need into steps, name the stakeholder at each step, and only then decide what role AI plays in each one

There are three roles to choose from: Automation (AI does it and you check), Augmentation (you and AI work on it together) and Agency (AI acts with latitude inside boundaries you set)

This produces a small table, one row per step, and the AI decision is made separately for each row

Asking "should we use AI here?" about the whole system skips that step-by-step choice.

Three more questions belong in every first session, because no model can be recommended without the answers:

  • ▸
    Capabilities. What specific features or capabilities must the model have to meet the customer's needs?
  • ▸
    Speed. How quickly does the model need to respond inside the customer's application?
  • ▸
    Cost. What is the budget, for development and for production usage?

Each answer comes from the customer's own workload

Facts such as how many people work in IT, which social platforms marketing uses, or the version number of an internal ticketing tool change none of the three

They tell you nothing about what the model must do, how fast it must answer, or what it may cost.

⚠️ จุดที่ข้อสอบชอบหลอก & จุดสังเกต (Exam Trap & Key Insight)

On the Exam: When asked which factors to establish before recommending a model, choose capabilities, response speed, and budget for development and production. Answers about organisational trivia are distractors, even when they sound like due diligence.

💡 Key Takeaway (หลักการสำคัญ):

The process map gives one row per step, and each row gets its own role. In Automation AI does the work and you check it. In Augmentation you and AI work on it together. In Agency AI acts with latitude inside limits you set. You choose a role for each step, not one answer to "should we use AI?" for the whole system.

คำแปลไทย: แผนผังกระบวนการให้หนึ่งแถวต่อขั้นตอน และแต่ละแถวจะได้รับบทบาทของตัวเอง ในระบบอัตโนมัติ AI ทำงานและคุณตรวจสอบ ใน Augmentation คุณและ AI ทำงานร่วมกัน ใน Agency AI ดำเนินการโดยมีละติจูดอยู่ภายในขีดจำกัดที่คุณกำหนดไว้ คุณเลือกบทบาทสำหรับแต่ละขั้นตอน ไม่ใช่คำตอบเดียวสำหรับ "เราควรใช้ AI หรือไม่" สำหรับทั้งระบบ

Structured discovery is the work you do before designing anything. You collect a fixed set of facts about who the system serves and what limits it, so every later decision can be checked against them. A request such as "patients need to check wait times before coming in" is where the conversation starts, not a requirement. To turn it into a brief, answer four questions in plain language:

- Users. Be specific. Which patients? What about the staff? Who else touches the system? - Needs. What outcome are they hoping for? This is not the same as the feature they asked for. - Experience. What does a great solution feel like to use? Describe the experience, not the features. - Constraints. Budget, the technical skill of the people who will run it, users' access to devices, and privacy requirements.

A process map is the discovery technique that keeps the brief honest. The idea that [you start from the business process, not the model](lesson:1.1) becomes a concrete step in discovery. Break the user's need into steps, name the stakeholder at each step, and only then decide what role AI plays in each one. There are three roles to choose from: Automation (AI does it and you check), Augmentation (you and AI work on it together) and Agency (AI acts with latitude inside boundaries you set). This produces a small table, one row per step, and the AI decision is made separately for each row. Asking "should we use AI here?" about the whole system skips that step-by-step choice.

Three more questions belong in every first session, because no model can be recommended without the answers:

- Capabilities. What specific features or capabilities must the model have to meet the customer's needs? - Speed. How quickly does the model need to respond inside the customer's application? - Cost. What is the budget, for development and for production usage?

Each answer comes from the customer's own workload. Facts such as how many people work in IT, which social platforms marketing uses, or the version number of an internal ticketing tool change none of the three. They tell you nothing about what the model must do, how fast it must answer, or what it may cost.

Discovery findings point to one of two starting paths. Model trade-offs in general are covered in [selecting Claude models on trade-offs](lesson:2.1). The skill here is reading the brief and seeing which path it already implies.

Both paths also use a second dial, effort. Effort is a per-request setting that controls how much reasoning the model does before it answers. A higher level buys more intelligence and costs latency and tokens; a lower level answers faster and cheaper but thinks less. That is why "the deepest reasoning setting" suits a hard problem and wastes money on simple triage.

Claude Haiku 4.5 suits the first path because it was built for interactive applications and high-volume processing. A support desk triaging 50,000 short tickets a day under a sub-second target is that workload. Starting there and testing on real ticket samples answers every finding at once. Starting with maximum reasoning on every request, or picking a model because of its context window, answers questions the customer never raised. Context window size is not one of the three criteria, and short triage prompts do not need a larger one.

The second path fits the opposite brief. Take a research team that needs multi-step literature synthesis, says accuracy outweighs cost, and wants a long-running agent that plans its own steps. Here, any proposal whose main purpose is lowering cost misses the stated priority. That includes a cheaper model, lower effort, or a batch pipeline. Tuning effort is a real lever, but it comes later: you adjust it on a model you have already chosen, after evals show where you stand. Whichever path you take, the decision is confirmed the same way, by testing with the customer's actual prompts and data.

The techniques in this section are run with Claude, acting as a coding agent that helps build the solution, before it writes any code. Claude can search a codebase and the internet quickly, so it finds your unknowns faster than you can alone. Some requirements cannot be collected by asking, because nobody knows to state them. Unknown unknowns are the questions you do not know to ask. In unfamiliar territory you may not know what good looks like, what has been tried before, or what potholes to avoid. Unknown knowns are criteria people only recognise when they see them, such as a layout or a visual style. Both are cheapest to find before implementation. Small changes in a feature or spec can produce drastically different code, and the coding agent finds earlier changes harder to revert.

Prototypes and mocks are spikes in practice. Each is a small throwaway build that answers one question, such as whether this layout works, without committing to a backend route or new state. An interview does the same for spoken requirements. It works best when you give Claude context about where you are and tell it to dig into the hard parts rather than the obvious ones.

Reviewing sample data exposes what interviews miss. Real inputs come in shapes nobody mentions in a meeting: irrelevant or nonexistent data, overly long input, poor or harmful user input, and ambiguous cases where even humans would struggle to agree. Asking the customer for a sample of production inputs early lets those cases shape the requirements, instead of appearing first in production.

The Description Chain traces every requirement through four links: the user's own words, a product requirement, a technical spec, and finally the instruction Claude receives. Prompt engineering is only the last link. The builder translates at every step, and each step involves judgement calls the previous one did not make. The chain also has a hard limit: Claude cannot hear what the user did not say.

The chain is a diagnostic tool as well as a writing order. If the code works but the product does not, that is a description failure, and the fix belongs at the link that broke, not in the prompt. Tests are the most precise form of description, which makes them a useful signal. A passing test with an unhappy user means the test described the wrong intent. The break is upstream, between the user's words and the requirement.
